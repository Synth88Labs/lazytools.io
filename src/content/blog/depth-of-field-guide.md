**That gorgeous blurred background — the one that makes a subject pop — is a shallow depth of field, and it's controlled by just four things.** Master them and you can dial in exactly how much of a photo is sharp: a paper-thin slice for a dreamy portrait, or everything from the flowers at your feet to the distant peaks.

<aside class="key-takeaways">

**Key takeaways**

- **Depth of field** = the zone that looks sharp. Shallow = blurry background.
- **Four controls:** aperture, focal length, subject distance, sensor size.
- **Blurry background:** wide aperture (low f-number), long lens, get close, big sensor.
- **Everything sharp:** small aperture (f/8–f/16), wide lens, step back, focus at the hyperfocal distance.
- Phones blur *in software* because their tiny sensors have huge depth of field.

</aside>

## What "depth of field" actually means

Depth of field (DoF) is the range of distances in a scene that appears acceptably sharp. Only a single plane is ever in *perfect* focus; everything in front of and behind it gets progressively softer. The zone where that softening is small enough to still read as "sharp" is your depth of field. Make that zone thin and the background dissolves into blur; make it deep and everything from the foreground to the horizon looks crisp.

Because "acceptably sharp" is a judgement, not a hard edge, DoF is defined against a **circle of confusion** — the largest blur spot that still looks like a point at normal viewing size. A common convention sets it to the sensor diagonal divided by about 1500, which is the value our calculators use. That is why the same lens can look "sharp enough" in a small web image and slightly soft in a large print: the standard assumes a typical print and viewing distance.

Four things move that zone, and nothing else does. Here they are in order of how directly you can reach for them.

## 1. Aperture — the big one

The aperture is the adjustable opening in the lens, measured in f-numbers. It's the most direct control: a **wide aperture (a low f-number like f/1.8)** gives a **shallow** depth of field and a soft background; a **small aperture (a high f-number like f/16)** keeps far more in focus. Confusingly, smaller f-numbers mean bigger openings — f/1.8 is a wide aperture, f/16 is a narrow one. Start here: to blur a background, open up.

Each full stop halves or doubles the light *and* changes the depth of field. As a rough feel, moving from f/2.8 to f/5.6 (two stops) roughly doubles the depth of field for the same shot. Worked example: a 50mm lens on a full-frame camera, subject 2 m away, gives a total depth of field of only a few centimetres at f/1.8, but well over a foot once you stop down to f/8 — same lens, same distance, just a smaller opening.

## 2. Focal length — longer blurs more

For the same aperture and subject size in the frame, a **longer lens gives a shallower depth of field**. An 85mm portrait lens throws a background far more out of focus than a 24mm wide-angle at the same f-stop. It's a big reason portrait photographers reach for 50mm, 85mm and 135mm lenses.

There is a subtlety worth knowing: if you keep the subject the *same size in the frame* — stepping back as you zoom in — the near-to-far focus zone changes only modestly with focal length. What the long lens really does is magnify and compress the *background*, so out-of-focus areas are blown up larger and read as much blurrier. In practice the effect is the same: reach for a longer lens when you want a creamier background.

## 3. Subject distance — get close

**The closer you focus, the shallower the depth of field.** Move in tight on your subject and the background dissolves; back away and more comes into focus. This is why macro photos have razor-thin focus — at a few centimetres, the depth of field can be millimetres. It also means *distance between the subject and the background* matters: the farther the background, the blurrier it renders. A portrait shot against a wall a foot away will never blur like the same portrait shot in an open field with the trees fifty metres back.

## 4. Sensor size — bigger blurs more

For the **same framing**, a **larger sensor gives a shallower depth of field**. A full-frame camera blurs backgrounds far more easily than a phone, because to get the same field of view a phone uses a very short lens — and short lenses have enormous depth of field. That's the whole reason your **phone fakes the blur** with a software "portrait mode" instead of doing it optically. The [crop factor calculator](/photography/crop-factor-calculator/) shows the equivalent aperture across formats.

The shorthand photographers use is *equivalent aperture*: multiply the f-number by the crop factor to compare blur across formats. An f/2.8 lens on Micro Four Thirds (crop factor ~2) blurs the background about like f/5.6 on full frame. It still gathers f/2.8 worth of light, but the *look* of the background matches the deeper f/5.6 on a bigger sensor.

## Quick reference: which way to push each control

| Control | For a blurry background | For front-to-back sharpness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aperture | Wide — f/1.4, f/1.8, f/2.8 | Narrow — f/8 to f/16 |
| Focal length | Longer — 85mm, 135mm | Wider — 16mm, 24mm, 35mm |
| Subject distance | Close to subject, background far away | Step back; keep the scene distant |
| Sensor / format | Larger — full frame, medium format | Smaller sensors help, or just stop down |
| Where to focus | On the subject's near eye | At the hyperfocal distance |

## The two recipes

Put the four together and you get a simple recipe for either extreme:

- **For a blurry background:** wide aperture (low f-number) · long lens · get close · big sensor · lots of distance to the background.
- **For everything sharp** (landscapes): small aperture (f/8–f/16) · wide lens · step back · and focus at the **hyperfocal distance** rather than the horizon, to squeeze out the maximum front-to-back sharpness.

### A worked portrait

Say you want a classic headshot with a melted background. Reach for an 85mm lens, open up to f/1.8 or f/2, and stand close enough to fill the frame with head and shoulders on a full-frame body. Place your subject well away from the background — a few metres is plenty. Focus on the near eye. The result is a razor-thin plane of sharpness across the face with a wash of soft colour behind.

### A worked landscape

Now the opposite. Fit a 24mm lens, stop down to f/11, and instead of focusing on the distant mountains, focus at the hyperfocal distance — the closest point you can focus while still keeping the horizon acceptably sharp. Everything from roughly half that distance out to infinity lands inside the depth of field, so the pebbles at your feet and the ridge line are both crisp. Going past f/16 usually backfires: diffraction begins to soften the whole frame, so f/8–f/11 is the sweet spot on most lenses.

**To convert an oven recipe to an air fryer, lower the temperature by about 25 °F (≈15 °C) and cut the cooking time by roughly 20% — and apply both changes together.** That single adjustment covers most recipes: an air fryer is really a small, powerful convection oven, so its fast-moving hot air cooks quicker and browns harder than a conventional oven. This guide gives you the rule, a ready-to-use oven to air fryer chart, and a worked example, plus the [air fryer conversion calculator](/cooking/air-fryer-conversion-calculator/) that does the math for you in your browser.

## The air fryer conversion rule

The most widely used rule of thumb has two parts, and you apply both at once:

1. **Lower the temperature by about 25 °F (≈15 °C).** So a 400 °F oven setting becomes roughly 375 °F in the air fryer.
2. **Cut the cooking time by about 20%.** So 30 minutes becomes roughly 24 minutes.

Why both? An air fryer's fan drives hot air around a small chamber, so the surface of the food heats faster (that's the time cut) and browns more aggressively (that's the temperature cut). Lower only the temperature and the food can still overcook because it's cooking so much quicker; shorten only the time and the outside can scorch before the inside is done. Together, the two adjustments keep the browning and the internal cooking roughly in balance — which is what you want from a good roast, a crisp tray of vegetables, or a batch of fries.

A quick way to remember it: **round the temperature down one dial notch and knock about a fifth off the clock.** For most home ovens, one common notch is 25 °F, and a fifth of the time is close enough to the 20% figure that you don't need precise arithmetic to get started.

Treat this as a **starting point, not an exact formula**. The −25 °F, −20% figure is the widely used rule of thumb, not a law of physics. Air fryers differ a lot in wattage and basket size — a small 1,000-watt basket model and a large 1,800-watt oven-style unit will not behave identically — so the first time you make a dish, check for doneness a few minutes before the calculated time is up. Once you've seen how your particular machine behaves, you can nudge the numbers up or down and trust them for next time.

## Why air fryers cook faster

An air fryer isn't a deep fryer and it isn't magic — it's a compact convection oven. A heating element sits close to the food and a fan circulates the hot air rapidly through a small basket. Two things follow from that design:

- **Speed.** In a big conventional oven, a lot of energy goes into heating a large volume of air and the oven walls. In an air fryer, the chamber is tiny and the fan is fast, so hot air reaches the food's surface quickly and constantly. That's why you can shave off around a fifth of the time.
- **Browning.** Fast-moving hot air pulls moisture off the surface efficiently, so food crisps and browns faster. That extra browning power is exactly why you dial the temperature down — otherwise the outside gets ahead of the inside.

Understanding this also explains the rule's limits: the air needs room to move. Crowd the basket and you lose both the speed and the crisping, because the food shields itself from the moving air and starts to steam in its own moisture. It's the same reason a single layer of chips crisps beautifully while a heaped basket comes out pale and soft. When a recipe makes more than fits in one comfortable layer, the fix is simple — cook it in two or three batches rather than trying to squeeze it all in at once. The extra few minutes are worth it for an even, crisp result across the whole batch.

## Foods that need extra care

The rule works well for the things air fryers are famous for — anything you'd normally roast or bake to a crisp finish. A few categories need a lighter touch:

- **Very saucy or liquid dishes** (stews, brothy bakes, anything with a lot of loose liquid) don't transfer well. Rushing air can't do much with a pool of sauce, and splatter is a real risk. These are usually better left in the oven.
- **Delicate batters** (thin, wet, unset batters) can blow around or cook unevenly in the moving air. If you're unsure, the oven is the safer bet.
- **Meat and poultry.** The conversion gets you close on timing, but for anything where safety matters, confirm doneness with an instant-read thermometer against a [safe internal temperature](https://www.usda.gov/). **Temperature, not time, is the true test.** If you're roasting a joint or a bird, our [roast cooking time calculator](/cooking/roast-cooking-time-calculator/) and [turkey cooking time calculator](/cooking/turkey-cooking-time-calculator/) give you weight-based timing and safe internal temperatures to aim for.

## Common mistakes

A handful of small habits trip people up when they first move recipes into the air fryer:

- **Overcrowding the basket.** This is the number one mistake. Air circulation is the whole point — pile food in and it steams instead of crisping. Leave space, or cook in batches.
- **Forgetting to shake or flip.** Food sits in one spot in the basket, so shake it (for small pieces) or flip it (for larger ones) about halfway through for even cooking and colour.
- **Not checking early.** Because machines vary, always check for doneness a few minutes before the calculated time on your first attempt. You can always cook longer; you can't un-cook.
- **Converting saucy dishes.** As above — loose, liquid recipes are the wrong fit. Don't force them.
- **Trusting time over a thermometer for meat.** The clock is a guide; the internal temperature is the answer. For anything where undercooking is a food-safety issue, check the temperature.

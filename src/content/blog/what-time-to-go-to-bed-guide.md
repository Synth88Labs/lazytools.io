**To wake up at 6:00 AM feeling rested, go to bed at about 10:15 PM.** That gives you five 90-minute sleep cycles (7.5 hours) plus roughly 15 minutes to fall asleep — and, crucially, it lands your alarm at the *end* of a cycle rather than the middle of one. Waking between cycles, in light sleep, is what leaves you clear-headed instead of groggy. It's why a well-timed 7.5 hours can feel better than a badly-timed 8.5.

> General sleep information, not medical advice. If you regularly can't fall or stay asleep, snore heavily, or feel exhausted in the day, see a doctor.

<aside class="key-takeaways">

**Key takeaways**

- **A sleep cycle averages ~90 minutes** (moving through light → deep → REM sleep).
- **Count back in 90-minute blocks** from your wake time, then add ~15 min to fall asleep.
- **5 cycles (7.5 h)** suits most adults; **6 cycles (9 h)** if you can. **4 cycles (6 h)** is a short-night minimum.
- **Waking at a cycle boundary** avoids "sleep inertia" — the heavy, groggy feeling.
- **Adults 18–64 need 7–9 hours**; teens 8–10; older adults 7–8.
- A round number of hours matters less than **waking between cycles**.

</aside>

## The 90-minute rule, in one line

During the night you don't sleep at one steady depth — you move through **[cycles of roughly 90 minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep_cycle)**, each running from light sleep down into deep sleep, up into REM (dreaming), and back. You repeat that four to six times a night.

The practical upshot: **plan your night in 90-minute blocks, and aim to wake at the top of a cycle.** Being woken from *deep* sleep, mid-cycle, is what produces "sleep inertia" — the disoriented, heavy grogginess that can linger for half an hour or more. Being woken from *light* sleep, at a cycle boundary, barely registers.

## What time should I go to bed? (Bedtime chart)

Working back from your wake-up time, allowing 15 minutes to fall asleep:

| Wake up at | 6 cycles (9 h) | **5 cycles (7.5 h)** | 4 cycles (6 h) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6:00 AM | 8:45 PM | **10:15 PM** | 11:45 PM |
| 6:30 AM | 9:15 PM | **10:45 PM** | 12:15 AM |
| 7:00 AM | 9:45 PM | **11:15 PM** | 12:45 AM |
| 7:30 AM | 10:15 PM | **11:45 PM** | 1:15 AM |
| 8:00 AM | 10:45 PM | **12:15 AM** | 1:45 AM |

The bold **5-cycle** column is the sweet spot for most adults. Pick 6 cycles if your schedule allows; drop to 4 only on a short night. If you fall asleep faster or slower than 15 minutes, adjust — the calculator lets you set your own sleep latency.

## What time to wake up if I go to bed now

Going the other way is just as useful when you're already tired. From the moment you're actually *asleep*, add 90-minute blocks:

| Fall asleep at | 4 cycles (6 h) | **5 cycles (7.5 h)** | 6 cycles (9 h) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10:00 PM | 4:00 AM | **5:30 AM** | 7:00 AM |
| 11:00 PM | 5:00 AM | **6:30 AM** | 8:00 AM |
| 12:00 AM | 6:00 AM | **7:30 AM** | 9:00 AM |

Set your alarm for one of these rather than a random time, and the last thing your night does is lift you out of light sleep.

## How much sleep do I need? (By age)

Cycle timing tells you *when* to wake; total need tells you *how many* cycles to aim for. The widely-used guidance:

| Age group | Recommended sleep |
| --- | --- |
| Newborns (0–3 months) | 14–17 hours |
| Infants (4–12 months) | 12–16 hours (incl. naps) |
| Toddlers (1–2 years) | 11–14 hours |
| Preschool (3–5 years) | 10–13 hours |
| School age (6–13 years) | 9–12 hours |
| Teens (14–17 years) | 8–10 hours |
| **Adults (18–64 years)** | **7–9 hours** |
| Older adults (65+) | 7–8 hours |

For most adults that's the 5-to-6-cycle range. Consistently getting less isn't something you "catch up" on fully at the weekend — the debt accumulates.

## Why do I wake up groggy even after 8 hours?

This is the question the cycle model answers best. If your alarm goes off in the middle of a cycle, while you're in **deep sleep**, your brain is yanked out of its deepest state — and you get **sleep inertia**: reduced alertness, slowed thinking, and that "just been hit by a truck" fog. It typically clears in 15–30 minutes but can linger longer.

So an 8.5-hour night that ends mid-cycle can genuinely feel worse than a 7.5-hour night that ends *between* cycles. More hours in bed isn't automatically better sleep — **when** you wake matters too.

### How to wake up less groggy

- **Time your alarm to a cycle boundary** using the bedtimes above — often just a 15–20 minute shift.
- **Get bright light immediately** on waking (open the curtains, or step outside) — it's the strongest "wake up now" signal to your body clock. A sunrise alarm that brightens gradually helps too.
- **Don't hit snooze.** Drifting back to sleep starts a new cycle you'll interrupt again minutes later — often making grogginess worse.
- **Move.** A short walk or even standing up and stretching raises alertness quickly. Cold water on the face helps.
- **Keep a consistent wake time**, even at weekends — a steady rhythm makes waking easier than any trick.

## Sleep hygiene that makes the cycles work

The calculator gives you target times, but the times only help if you actually fall asleep near them. The fundamentals:

- **Screens off ~60–90 minutes before bed** — blue light suppresses melatonin, delaying sleep onset.
- **Keep the room cool and dark** — around 60–67°F (16–19°C) suits most people. (A comfortable room also depends on humidity — see the [dew point guide](/blog/dew-point-vs-humidity-guide/).)
- **Consistent schedule** — same bedtime and wake time daily trains your body to be ready.
- **Watch late caffeine and alcohol** — caffeine lingers for hours; alcohol helps you fall asleep but fragments the second half of the night.

**To turn an hourly wage into a yearly salary, multiply your hourly rate by the hours you work
per week, then by 52 weeks: annual salary = hourly rate × hours/week × 52.** So $25 an hour at
40 hours a week is $25 × 40 × 52 = **$52,000 a year** — about $1,000 a week and $4,333 a month.
This *hourly to salary* math is the same for any currency; the [hourly to salary
calculator](/calc/hourly-to-salary-calculator/) does it instantly, and everything below shows the
logic so you can check it in your head.

## The hourly-to-salary formula

Everything comes from one line:

**annual salary = hourly rate × hours per week × 52**

A standard year has 52 weeks, so if you know how much you earn in one hour and how many hours you
work in a week, you multiply out to a year. The two intermediate figures are handy too:

- **Weekly pay** = hourly rate × hours per week
- **Monthly pay** = annual salary ÷ 12

Take $25 an hour at 40 hours a week:

- Weekly: $25 × 40 = **$1,000**
- Annual: $1,000 × 52 = **$52,000**
- Monthly: $52,000 ÷ 12 ≈ **$4,333**

Change any input and the whole chain updates. At 30 hours a week instead of 40, the same $25 rate
gives $25 × 30 × 52 = $39,000 a year. Part-time hours, a second rate, a raise — it's always the
same multiplication.

Why 52 specifically? A calendar year is 365 days, which is 52 weeks plus one extra day (two in a
leap year). Payroll almost always works in whole weeks, so 52 is the standard multiplier and the
stray day or two is absorbed into how employers schedule pay periods. If you're paid every two
weeks you'll see 26 paychecks a year; twice a month, 24. The annual total lands in the same place
either way.

One thing the formula quietly assumes: that you're paid for all 52 weeks. If you take unpaid time
off or only work part of the year, use your *actual* paid weeks instead. Working 50 weeks at $25 ×
40 is $50,000, not $52,000. Enter your real hours and weeks to model that.

## The ×2,000 mental shortcut

You don't always have a calculator open. At a 40-hour week there's a shortcut worth memorizing.

A full 40-hour year is 52 × 40 = **2,080 hours**. So:

**annual ≈ hourly rate × 2,080**

That's exact for 40 hours a week. But 2,080 is awkward to multiply in your head, so round it to
**2,000** — which is the same as doubling the rate and adding three zeros:

- $25/hr → about **$50,000** (25 × 2,000)
- $50/hr → about **$100,000**
- $18/hr → about **$36,000**

The ×2,000 version runs a little low — roughly 4% under the true number — because you dropped the
last 80 hours. It's perfect for a quick gut check ("is this job worth roughly $50k or $75k?") but
reach for ×2,080, or the calculator, when you need the exact figure.

Here's what common wages come to at **40 hours a week across all 52 weeks** (gross, before tax):

| Hourly rate | Weekly (× 40) | Annual (× 2,080) | ≈ Monthly (÷ 12) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $15 | $600 | $31,200 | $2,600 |
| $18 | $720 | $37,440 | $3,120 |
| $20 | $800 | $41,600 | $3,467 |

| $30 | $1,200 | $62,400 | $5,200 |
| $40 | $1,600 | $83,200 | $6,933 |
| $50 | $2,000 | $104,000 | $8,667 |

Notice how the ×2,000 shortcut lines up: $20 → "$40k" (actual $41,600), $30 → "$60k" (actual
$62,400). Always a touch low, always in the right ballpark.

## Convert a salary back to an hourly rate

To go the other way — you know the yearly salary and want the hourly equivalent — divide by your
paid hours in a year:

**hourly rate ≈ annual salary ÷ 2,080**

For a $75,000 salary at 40 hours a week:

$75,000 ÷ 2,080 ≈ **$36.06 an hour**

The mental version divides by 2,000 instead: $75,000 ÷ 2,000 = $37.50, which slightly *over*states
the rate (the mirror image of the ×2,000 shortcut running low the other direction). This reverse
conversion is how you compare a salaried offer against a contract or freelance rate — put both on
the same hourly footing first.

One thing to weigh when you do: a salary usually bundles paid time off, while an hourly or contract
rate often doesn't. If a $75,000 salaried role includes three paid weeks off, you're really earning
that $36.06 across 49 worked weeks — so an hourly contractor would need a higher headline rate to
match it once their own unpaid holidays are counted. The arithmetic gets you a like-for-like
starting point; the paid-time-off difference is the adjustment to make next.

## Common mistakes

A few traps turn a clean calculation into a wrong one:

- **Confusing gross with take-home.** Rate × hours × 52 is *gross* pay. Income tax, payroll
  deductions, and benefit contributions all come out before the money reaches you, so your actual
  take-home is lower. Budget from net, not from the headline annual figure.
- **Assuming 52 paid weeks.** Unpaid leave, furloughs, or a partial year mean you're not paid for
  all 52 weeks. Use your real number of paid weeks or the annual figure will be too high.
- **Ignoring overtime.** The formula uses one flat rate. If you work paid overtime at a higher
  rate, those hours earn more than the base calculation shows — add them separately.
- **Treating ×2,000 as exact.** The 2,000 shortcut is an approximation that runs about 4% low;
  the precise multiplier for a 40-hour week is 2,080. Know which one you're using and why.
